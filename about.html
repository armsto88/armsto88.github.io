About

An ecologist who builds the monitoring system, not just one part of it.

My work combines field ecology, software, electronics and audio to make environmental monitoring more practical and easier to interpret.

Background

From sound systems to ecological systems

My route into conservation technology began with audio engineering, then moved through field conservation and zoology into the design of ecological monitoring tools.

I trained in Audio Engineering and worked in live sound, location recording and post-production. That background gave me a practical understanding of microphones, recording systems and signal chains — knowledge that now carries directly into passive acoustic monitoring and bioacoustics.

From January 2023 to August 2024, I worked in bush regeneration and conservation fieldwork on the Sunshine Coast in Australia. The role included habitat restoration, species identification, bird surveys, Glossy-black Cockatoo monitoring and acoustic recorder deployment.

Alongside completing a Bachelor of Science in Zoology, I moved further into ecological software and hardware development. I now contribute to ecoPi at OekoFor and design a distributed environmental sensing system with the University of New England / REED Lab.

The common thread is practical integration: understanding the ecological question, the field constraints, the hardware, the data path and the interface used to interpret the result.

Approach

How I approach a monitoring problem

The ecological decision comes first. Hardware, software and automation are selected around that need and the realities of deployment.

  1. Define the ecological question

    Clarify what needs to be observed, what decision the evidence should support and what resolution is genuinely useful.

  2. Design for field constraints

    Work around power, weather, communications, servicing intervals and the people who will deploy and maintain the system.

  3. Close the data loop

    Plan storage, validation, review and interpretation alongside the hardware so observations become usable ecological information.

Working principles

Practical, legible and maintainable

  • Ecological usefulness is the benchmark, not technical novelty.
  • Field testing should begin early enough to shape the design.
  • Local data capture and clear failure states matter in remote deployments.
  • Interfaces are part of the monitoring method, not an afterthought.
  • Automation should remain reviewable and honest about uncertainty.
  • Clear systems are easier for teams to operate, diagnose and improve.
